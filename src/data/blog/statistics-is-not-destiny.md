My parents found out I was at elevated risk for Type 2 diabetes when I was in my twenties, at a routine physical. The doctor didn't run any unusual tests. He just told us what the research says: South Asians develop insulin resistance at lower BMIs, earlier in life, than white Europeans. The numbers were unambiguous. Our risk is something like two to four times higher, even controlling for weight. I remember my mother nodding gravely. I remember feeling like I had just been handed a sentence.

What I didn't understand then — and what took me years of working with data to really internalize — is that the sentence wasn't about me. It was about a distribution.

Here's what a finding like that actually means. Researchers study a large population. They find that people with a certain ancestry, on average, show certain metabolic tendencies. They quantify the difference. They publish. The finding is real. The finding is useful. It should absolutely inform clinical practice and public health policy. And then someone reads it, and they translate "Group X is Y times more likely to experience Z" into "I will experience Z." Those two things are not the same statement. One is about a distribution. The other is a prediction about a single draw from that distribution — which, statistically speaking, tells you almost nothing.

This is one of the oldest confusions in how people relate to statistics, and it never gets less strange to watch in practice. A study shows that people who skip breakfast are more likely to be overweight. So people reason: if I skip breakfast, I'll gain weight. But skipping breakfast doesn't cause weight gain. People who skip breakfast are different from people who don't in dozens of ways that actually drive the outcome. The statistic described a group. It said nothing causal about any individual's cereal habits.

Or take the famous studies on social mobility. There's a finding that children born into the bottom income quintile have a roughly one-in-ten chance of reaching the top quintile. Depressing, if you let it land that way. But think about what it actually tells you about any specific child. It tells you that in a large sample, most won't make it. It doesn't tell you which ones. It doesn't tell you what made the difference for those who did. It tells you about a distribution, and distributions don't have names.

The same logic applies across medicine, education, criminology, economics — anywhere we try to use aggregate data to say something meaningful about human lives. The pattern is always the same. The research is about populations. The reader is an individual. The translation is lossy in a way that statistics can't fix and that narrative almost never acknowledges.

I've been thinking about why this matters so much. Part of it is practical: if you treat a statistical risk as a personal verdict, you'll make worse decisions. Either you'll panic unnecessarily, or you'll fatalize, which is its own kind of distortion — well, the numbers say I'm cooked anyway, so why bother. Both responses are failures of reasoning. The number wasn't about you. It was about a group you resemble in one or two measurable ways, which is not the same as resembling in every way that matters.

But there's something deeper, too. Statistics about human populations have always been used to define what's possible for people. In the nineteenth century, Francis Galton — who coined the term "eugenics" and was genuinely enthusiastic about the project — was also one of the pioneers of modern statistical thinking. He was trying to use distributions to argue that some people were constitutively limited. The math has gotten more sophisticated. The impulse hasn't fully died. Every time a finding about group differences gets absorbed into how people think about themselves or others, there's a risk that it starts functioning like destiny — like the number is the person, not a rough contour of a very complicated landscape.

The better way to hold a statistical risk is as a prior, not a sentence. I'm South Asian, so I should probably pay attention to my metabolic health, get my blood sugar checked more often than the median recommendation, stay skeptical of advice calibrated to populations that don't look like me. The research is useful that way — as a prompt to be more attentive, not as a prediction about how things will go. The number shifts my priors. It doesn't write my future.

What strikes me, having spent years building models for a living, is how easy it is to forget this even when you know it. You can understand the epistemology perfectly well and still feel, viscerally, like a finding about your group is a finding about you. I think that's partly because we're wired to personalize — to take any pattern we notice and connect it to our specific situation. Statistics fights against that. It's a technology for thinking about populations, and it requires real effort to not let population-level truths collapse into individual predictions.

The finding about South Asian metabolic risk is true. It is a genuine signal in noisy data, and ignoring it would be foolish. I take it seriously. I also know, with about as much certainty as I can have, that it doesn't tell me what's going to happen. It tells me something about a large group of people I belong to in one particular way. What happens to me depends on dozens of things that number never measured. That's not a comforting story exactly — it would be nice to have a cleaner map — but it's the honest one.

The map is not the territory. The distribution is not the person. Statistics describes what's true in aggregate. The rest, for better and worse, is still open.
